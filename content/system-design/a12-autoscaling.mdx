Traffic changes through the day. Running enough servers for the busiest minute of the year wastes money the rest of the time; running too few fails at the worst moment. **Autoscaling** adds capacity when load rises and removes it when load falls.

## How an autoscaler decides

The autoscaler in the lab works like most real ones. Every couple of seconds it looks at how busy the web servers were. Above 70%, it starts more workers; below 30% for a while, it removes some. The workers setting is the minimum it will keep running.

The crucial detail: new workers aren't ready instantly. A real server has to start, load code, warm up caches and pass health checks. In the lab that takes 10 seconds.

## A slow ramp: autoscaling works

Turn autoscaling on, then ramp traffic up to 400 requests a second over 30 seconds. Watch the web servers: as they get busy, more workers start, and capacity climbs with the traffic. Nothing fails, because the traffic grew slower than new workers could arrive.

## A sudden spike: autoscaling is too late

Switch to spikes. Each spike quadruples traffic for 2 seconds. The autoscaler notices, starts workers, and they arrive 10 seconds later, long after the spike has passed and errors have already happened. Then they sit idle until they're removed again.

An autoscaler can only respond to change that's slower than its start-up time.

## Getting through spikes anyway

- **Headroom**: keep enough spare capacity running at all times to absorb the biggest spike you expect. It costs money every hour, which is the trade-off.
- **Scheduled scaling**: if you know the spike is coming (a sale at 9 am, a match kick-off), scale up beforehand.
- **Faster start-up**: smaller services, pre-built images and warm pools of ready servers cut start time from minutes to seconds.
- **A queue**: if the work can wait a little, let a queue absorb the spike and drain it afterwards, as in the queues lesson.

<Note>
Autoscaling also has to scale down carefully. Remove capacity too eagerly and the next bump in traffic triggers another scale-up: the system **flaps** up and down. Waiting a while before removing workers avoids that.
</Note>

## What to remember

- Autoscaling matches capacity to traffic, but always reacts after the fact.
- New capacity takes time to start; changes faster than that aren't helped.
- Headroom, scheduled scaling, faster start-up or a queue cover the gap.
- Scale down slowly to avoid flapping.
